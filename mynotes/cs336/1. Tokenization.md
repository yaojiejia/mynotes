## Part 1 — Why build from scratch

### The problem the course exists to solve
Researchers have drifted up the abstraction ladder: **implement your own model (~2015) → download BERT and fine-tune (~2018) → just prompt (today)**. Prompting is fine and powerful, but **abstractions are leaky** — when the model can't do the thing you want, you have no recourse. Fundamental research needs the whole stack torn open.

### But the frontier is out of reach
- GPT-4 reportedly ~$100M to train (2023); frontier runs now speculated near ~$1B.
- No public details — the GPT-4 paper explicitly declines to describe how the model was built, citing competitive landscape and safety.
- So we build *small* models. **Caveat: small models are not miniature frontier models.**

Two concrete ways small-scale intuitions fail to transfer:
1. **Flop distribution shifts with scale.** MLP layers ≈ 44% of flops at small scale, ≈ 80% at 175B. Optimizing attention at small scale may buy you nothing at large scale.
2. **Emergence.** Some few-shot capabilities sit flat at chance until a critical scale, then jump.

### What actually transfers — 3 kinds of knowledge
| Type | What it is | Transfers? |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanics** | What a Transformer is, how model parallelism works, how kernels work | ✅ Yes — teachable by construction |
| **Mindset** | Squeeze the hardware; profile & benchmark everything; take scaling seriously | ✅ Yes |
| **Intuitions** | Which data/modeling decisions actually help | ⚠️ Not reliably — needs scale + experiments |

On intuitions: some design choices are pure empiricism. Shazeer's SwiGLU paper concludes it offers *no explanation* and attributes the gains to "divine benevolence."

### The bitter lesson, correctly read
- ❌ Wrong: "scale is all that matters, algorithms don't."
- ✅ Right: **"algorithms that scale are what matter."**

```
accuracy ≈ efficiency × resources        (efficiency = output / input)
```

Efficiency matters *more* as scale grows. At small scale a 2× slowdown means you wait longer; at frontier scale it's hundreds of millions of dollars, and even 5% is a big deal. OpenAI (2020) measured **44× algorithmic efficiency gain on ImageNet, 2012→2019** — on top of hardware gains; the two multiply.

**Framing for the whole course:** *what is the best model you can build with a given data and compute budget?* For pretraining, usually compute-bound (data assumed plentiful) — but that could flip.

## Part 2 — Brief history

- **Shannon (1950s)** — LMs used to measure the entropy of English.
- **N-gram era** — a *component* inside MT and speech-recognition systems, ensuring fluent output.
- **Neural lineage:** LSTMs (90s) → Bengio's first neural LM (2003 — just a feedforward net over a small context) → seq2seq ("compress a whole sentence into a vector") → Adam, attention (built for MT), the Transformer (also built for MT) → MoE, model parallelism (2010s).
- **Late 2010s:** ELMo / BERT — pretrain then fine-tune. Google's work foreshadowed "prompt in, response out."
- **OpenAI opens the floodgates** by embracing scaling: GPT → GPT-2 → **scaling laws** → GPT-3 (>10× the largest model at the time; in-context learning emerges).
- Google's response was undertrained; DeepMind (then separate) worked out **compute-optimal** scaling laws.
- **Replication wave:** EleutherAI (open datasets/models, compute-limited), Meta's 175B OPT (hardware pain, weak), HuggingFace BigScience. All fairly weak.
- **Last ~3 years:** open weights close the gap — Llama 1/2/3, Mistral, then the Chinese labs (DeepSeek, Qwen, and others). Credible, widely used in industry.
- **Fully open** (weights + paper + code + data): AI2, NVIDIA, **Marin** (Percy's project). Data mixture is the piece most commonly withheld.

**Why the open ecosystem matters:** this course would be impossible without it. Papers on big MoE and RL systems let us triangulate how frontier models are built.

### What an "LM" means keeps changing
fine-tune it → prompt it → **talk to it** (ChatGPT era) → **agents** (today). What we demand now was unimaginable 10 years ago — yet the fundamentals barely moved: GPUs + kernels, SGD-like optimization, Transformers + attention. The *specs* changed: much longer context ⇒ **inference efficiency matters far more**.

### A1 — Basics
Tokenizer (BPE) + Transformer + loss + optimizer + full training step, plus **resource accounting** (know where every flop goes). Train on TinyStories / OpenWebText. Leaderboard: drive perplexity down fastest (NanoGPT-speedrun flavored).

**The unifying tension of A1** — everything is balancing three things:
- **Expressivity** (represent the complexity of the data)
- **Stability** (keep parameter & gradient norms in the goldilocks zone — a huge fraction of LM training is just *stability*)
- **Efficiency** (run fast on hardware)

Architecture choices are usually "make it cheaper by projecting into a lower dimension — but does it still work?" That trade-off *is* the game.

Things that evolved past the original Transformer: activations, positional encodings, normalization, attention variants (attention is O(n²)), SSMs / linear attention (Mamba, Gated DeltaNet — hybrids with attention work well), **MoE now dominant** for compute-efficient MLPs, and *shape* (layers / heads / hidden dim / #experts) which sounds trivial but has huge implications.

Training-side knobs: next-token vs **multi-token prediction**, AdamW vs **Muon** (used in recent open models like Kimi K2), init (big effect on large-model stability), LR schedule, regularization, batch size, MoE-specific tricks. These *look* like hyperparameters, but principled settings are the difference between a run that blows up and a SOTA run.

### A2 — Systems
Resource accounting (**≈ 6ND flops** to train an N-param model on D tokens — derived in lecture 2), hardware model, **roofline analysis** (compute-bound vs memory-bound — usually memory), benchmarking/profiling.

- **Key picture:** memory is not where compute is. You move params/activations from memory → compute → back. That movement is usually the bottleneck. B200 ≈ 2.25 PFLOP/s bf16 vs ≈ 8 TB/s memory bandwidth.
- **Kernels** (Triton): a kernel is a function that runs on the GPU; PyTorch ops already launch built-in ones. Principle: **organize compute to minimize data movement** → operator **fusion** (read once, do both ops, write once) and **tiling**.
- **Multi-GPU**: same principle, but cross-GPU movement is even more expensive. Collectives (gather / reduce / all-reduce). Shard params, activations, gradients, optimizer state — by data, model, layer, sequence, or expert; each with trade-offs. DGX B200 = 8 GPUs on NVLink; scale out over InfiniBand/Ethernet.
- **Inference**: needed for chat, RL rollouts, test-time compute, synthetic data, eval. Two phases — **prefill** (whole prompt forward, builds KV pairs, training-like) and **decode** (one token at a time, quickly **memory-bound** — this is why inference is hard). Speedups: pruning, quantization, distillation, **speculative decoding** (cheap model guesses ahead, big model verifies in parallel), inference-specific kernels, and request **batching** (unlike training, arrival times are unpredictable).
- 📖 Recommended: *How to Scale Your Model* (Google) — great on roofline + Transformer math. TPU-centric but has a GPU chapter now.

### A3 — Scaling laws
Setting: you have 1e25 FLOPs (tens of millions of dollars). You get **one** run. You can't hyperparameter-tune at that scale.

**Conceptual shift: don't design a model, design a *scaling recipe*** — a mapping from FLOP budget → hyperparameter config. Run small-scale experiments, fit a scaling law, **predict the loss at the target scale**. That lets you (a) optimize the recipe cheaply and (b) predict results before spending the money (useful for fundraising).

> **Scaling laws are not laws of nature — you have to will them into existence** via careful recipe construction. The recipe must *extrapolate* (how does LR move with scale? batch size?).

- **Hyperparameter transfer**: parameterize so small-scale HPs are either reused directly or a predictable function of scale. Otherwise you can't guess the right LR at the big scale.
- **Predictability is at least as important as optimality.**
- Classic result (Kaplan / **Chinchilla**): for each FLOP budget, sweep model sizes, take the min, fit a curve. Rule of thumb **≈ 20 tokens per parameter** (70B → ~1.4T tokens); varies with dataset and architecture. Points falling on a line = confidence; scattered = none.
- Caveat: Chinchilla ignores **inference cost** — modern models are deliberately *smaller and over-trained* for cheap serving.
- Marin pre-registers scaling predictions publicly before the big run.
- **A3 itself:** a fake training API backed by a cache of pre-run models — submit configs, get losses back, fit scaling laws, extrapolate, get graded on where your final model lands. Simulates the high-stakes $100M budget decision, minus the stakes.

### A4 — Data
**Data quality basically determines how good the model is.** Data = a specification of what you want the model to do (multilingual? conversational? long agentic coding?).

- **Evaluation first** — it defines the target capability.
  - **Internal metrics** (model development): must be **smooth across scales**; *relative* performance is what matters (a perplexity of 1.2 means little in absolute terms).
  - **External metrics** (customers, reviewers): **ecological validity** matters. Conflating the two is a common mistake.
  - Prefer eval data not on the internet (contamination). Use *many diverse* evals — averaging into one number hides a lot.
- **Data doesn't fall from the sky — it's actively curated.** Web crawl, books (legally fraught), arXiv, GitHub. Legal issues are real: fair use on copyrighted data, licensing, and e.g. unlicensed GitHub code (assume permissive or be conservative?).
- Raw data isn't text — it's HTML, PDFs, directories. Pipeline: **transform → filter → dedupe → mix sources → (increasingly) synthetic data** (rewriting real data into more useful forms).
- Stages: **pretraining → mid-training** (high-quality data at the end; long-context data like big repos/books) → **post-training** (conversations, agentic traces with tool calls).
- **A4** = start from a raw web crawl and do the filtering/dedup yourself. Dirty work, but part of the full experience.

### A5 — Alignment
Pretraining is **full supervision** (predict the next token). Alignment adds **weak supervision** — because *critiquing is easier than generating*. Template: generate responses → score them (human / verifier / LM judge) → update the model to prefer better ones. Instantiated as PPO/GRPO, or DPO for preference data.

- RL is unstable and hard to tune; stay in the supervised regime as long as you can.
- **RL at scale is a systems problem**: separate inference and training servers, rollout orchestration (especially with code-execution environments), lagging workers → off-policy drift, constantly trading on-policyness against throughput.

### The through-line: everything is efficiency
Fixed resources (data, compute cores, memory, bandwidth) → best model per some eval.
- Systems → compute efficiency
- Tokenization → compute efficiency (raw bytes are too expensive for today's architectures)
- Architecture → reduce memory/flops, often driven by **inference** cost
- Data filtering → fixed budget means time on bad data is time stolen from good data
- Scaling laws → do the hyperparameter tuning on small models

# Part 4 — TOKENIZATION (the actual unit)

> 📺 Also recommended: Karpathy's tokenization video.

## The problem
Raw text is **Unicode strings**. A language model puts a distribution over **sequences of tokens** (integers). So we need a round-trippable pair of procedures.

```python
class Tokenizer(ABC):
    def encode(self, string: str) -> list[int]: ...
    def decode(self, indices: list[int]) -> str: ...
```

**Invariant: `decode(encode(s)) == s`. If your tokenizer doesn't round-trip, it's broken.**

Conceptually a tokenizer is a **segmentation of the text**.

## Compression ratio — the metric that matters

```
compression ratio = (# bytes) / (# tokens)     "bytes per token"
```

Example from lecture: a 20-byte string → 8 tokens → **2.5 bytes/token**.

- **Higher ratio = shorter sequences = good** (attention is quadratic in sequence length).
- You can always raise it by growing the vocab — but then each vocab entry is a *distinct* symbol seen rarely ⇒ **sparsity**. Modern (esp. multilingual) tokenizers land around **100k–200k** tokens.
- **This is the central trade-off: vocab size ⟷ sequence length.**

## Why tokenizers are annoying (playground observations)
- A word and *the same word with a leading space* are **different tokens** — you'll see many tokens that are literally `" word"`. `"hello"` and `" hello"` are unrelated indices.
- **Numbers** get chunked a few digits at a time, sometimes unpredictably. Some tokenizers force one digit per token, but that blows up sequence length.

## Four attempts

### 1. Character-level (Unicode code points)
`ord()` / `chr()`. ~150k possible characters.
- ❌ Vocab ~150k and most of it is rare — an inefficient use of the vocabulary; many indices are barely used.
- ❌ Compression ratio is poor, so sequences stay long.

### 2. Byte-level (UTF-8)
Every string → bytes; every value in **0–255**. `"a"` is 1 byte, other characters take several.
- ✅ Tiny vocab (256), nothing is ever out-of-vocabulary.
- ❌ **Compression ratio = 1.0 by definition.** Sequences are enormous — blows past the context length.

### 3. Word-level (regex / split on spaces) — the classic NLP approach
- ✅ Tokens are *meaningful* — humans invented words, and words have stable semantics. Compression ratio is decent.
- ❌ Vocab = number of distinct chunks in the training data — huge, and actually **unbounded**: at test time you'll hit a word you've never seen. The old fix was an `UNK` token, which is ugly and corrupts perplexity calculations.

### 4. ✅ Byte Pair Encoding (BPE)
Originally a **1994 data-compression** algorithm, long before LMs; brought to NLP for **neural machine translation**; first used for LLMs in **GPT-2**.

**Idea:** *train the tokenizer on raw text* so the vocabulary is tailored to the data.
- Common sequences → a single token.
- Rare sequences → split into smaller units. **No `UNK` ever** — you can always fall back to bytes.

## The BPE training algorithm

1. Start from the corpus as a **byte sequence** — each byte is a token (indices 0–255).
2. Count all **adjacent pairs** of tokens.
3. Take the **most frequent pair**.
4. **Merge** it: allocate a new index `256 + i`, record `merges[pair] = new_index` and `vocab[new_index] = vocab[a] + vocab[b]`, then replace every occurrence of that pair in the sequence.
5. Repeat for `num_merges` iterations.

```python
def train_bpe(string: str, num_merges: int) -> BPETokenizerParams:
    indices = list(map(int, string.encode("utf-8")))
    merges: dict[tuple[int, int], int] = {}          # pair -> new index
    vocab: dict[int, bytes] = {x: bytes([x]) for x in range(256)}

    for i in range(num_merges):
        counts = defaultdict(int)
        for a, b in zip(indices, indices[1:]):       # all adjacent pairs
            counts[(a, b)] += 1

        pair = max(counts, key=counts.get)           # most frequent pair
        new_index = 256 + i
        merges[pair] = new_index
        vocab[new_index] = vocab[pair[0]] + vocab[pair[1]]
        indices = merge(indices, pair, new_index)    # replace all occurrences

    return BPETokenizerParams(vocab=vocab, merges=merges)
```

`BPETokenizerParams` holds exactly two things: `vocab` (index → bytes) and `merges` (pair → merged index).

### Worked example — "the cat in the hat"
- Bytes: `t` = 116, `h` = 104, `e` = 101 …
- Most frequent pair is `(116, 104)` = `"th"` (appears twice, ties broken by first) → becomes **256**. Both occurrences are replaced.
- Next iteration merges `(256, 101)` = `"the"` → **257**.
- Next → **258**. And so on.
- **Each step: the sequence gets shorter, the vocabulary gets bigger.** Toy compression ratio ends around 1.5.

### Encoding new text
Walk through the learned merges in order and apply them to the byte sequence. Decoding just concatenates `vocab[i]` for each index — and must give the original string back.

## What A1 asks you to fix (the naive version is very slow)
- **`encode` loops over every merge** — and there are `vocab_size − 256` of them. You need to **only iterate over the merges that actually apply**, which means building indices/data structures over pair positions.
- **Special tokens** — conceptually shallow, but required for a real tokenizer.
- **Pre-tokenization**: don't run BPE on the whole string. **Split text into chunks first** (GPT-2 style regex), then tokenize each chunk. Much faster, and it stops merges from crossing word boundaries.
- At some point you'll notice **Python is just slow** — porting to Rust/C is fair game.

## Summary
- Tokenizers convert between strings and integer indices, and **must round-trip**.
- Character-, byte-, and word-level are each suboptimal in their own way (vocab too big / sequences too long / vocab unbounded).
- **BPE is an effective, data-driven heuristic** — that's really all it is.
- Percy says every year he hopes not to teach this. The dream is **end-to-end from raw bytes** (recent hierarchical/byte-level networks look promising) — but **nothing tokenizer-free has been scaled to the frontier**, so tokenizers stay.

### Whatever replaces tokenization must still satisfy:
1. **The Transformer needs to operate on some abstraction of the sequence**, not raw units. Clearest for video or DNA, where individual bytes have very low signal-to-noise — you have to lift into a space where modeling is possible.
2. **Chunks must be variable-length → adaptive computation.** Not all bytes deserve equal compute; a big boring span should compress to one token while the rare/interesting parts stay expanded. Without this you're leaving efficiency on the table.
